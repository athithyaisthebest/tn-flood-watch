How it works

Floods in Tamil Nadu usually follow very heavy rain over one to three days, often from a cyclone or depression during the north-east monsoon (October to December). The site looks for exactly that, district by district.

Step 1

Rain: forecast and observed

Every three hours: the rainfall forecast for the next three days, NASA satellite rainfall for the wettest spot in each district, and the heaviest reading from Tamil Nadu's ~590 rain gauges.

Step 2

Rivers and reservoirs

Seven-day river-flow forecasts on major rivers (Cauvery, Tamirabarani, Vaigai, Vellar, Adyar and more), and daily storage in the state's reservoirs: a nearly full, rising reservoir raises the level downstream.

Step 3

Compare with history

Each signal is compared with thresholds set from that district's own history since 2015 and tested on real floods. Single rain gauges use IMD's heavy-rain categories.

Step 4

Show the highest level

A district takes the highest level reached by any signal, and its panel shows which signals triggered it, so you can judge for yourself.

Track record

How well would this system have flagged Tamil Nadu's recent floods? Thresholds were set using floods from 2015 to 2021, then tested on floods from 2022 onwards that the system had never seen.

About and limitations

Why this exists

Tamil Nadu's official flood systems are excellent but cover parts of the state, such as Chennai or specific river gauges. This is a simple, open, state-wide view that publishes its own accuracy.

What it can't see

Street flooding from blocked drains, dam and lake releases, tank breaches, storm surge and very local cloudbursts. Rain is checked at one point per district, so a downpour can be missed.

Data

Forecasts from Open-Meteo (river flow: Copernicus GloFAS). Satellite rain: NASA GPM IMERG. Gauge-based history: IMD gridded rainfall. Rain gauges and reservoirs: TN-SMART, Government of Tamil Nadu. Boundaries: INDIAN-SHAPEFILES.
